    12. Writing a formal RCA¶

    Time: ~3 hours Prerequisites: 06, 07, 10, 11

    1. In plain words¶

    A root cause analysis (RCA) is the document that explains, after an incident, what happened, why it happened, and what you have done so it cannot happen again. For a bank, it is also a contract artefact: the support agreement usually requires one within a fixed time, the bank's IT risk team reviews it, and parts of it may go into their report to the regulator.

    Think of an aircraft accident report. It does not say "the plane crashed because it hit the ground". It says the pilot descended because the altimeter read wrong, because a sensor was iced, because the heater had failed, because the maintenance check did not include the heater. Each step has evidence. The fix is not "tell pilots to be careful"; it is "add the heater to the checklist and add an alarm". A good software RCA has the same shape: a chain of evidence from the symptom to the defect, from the defect to the process gap that let it ship, and fixes for both.

    The hard part is not the writing. It is being honest about what you proved and what you only believe, separating the thing that happened that night (the trigger) from the thing that was wrong all along (the root cause), and writing it so a bank's operations head and its chief architect both come away trusting your team more than before the incident.

    2. The real thing¶

    2.1 The structure¶

    Every RCA in this course uses this structure. Most banks and vendors use a variant of it; if the client has a template, use theirs and map these sections onto it.

    # Section What goes in Length
    0 Header Incident id, title, severity, service, environment, dates, author, reviewers, version, status (preliminary/final) table
    1 Executive summary What happened, impact, root cause, fix, in plain words. A reader who stops here knows everything important. 4-8 sentences
    2 Impact Who and what was affected, duration, counts, financial and data impact, SLA impact table + few lines
    3 Timeline Timestamped events from first symptom to resolution, with sources table
    4 Root cause The defect, the mechanism from defect to failure, the evidence 1-2 pages
    5 Trigger Why it happened that day a paragraph
    6 Contributing factors Conditions that made it possible, worse or slower to fix bullets
    7 5-whys The causal chain from symptom to process gap numbered list
    8 Resolution / fix Immediate mitigation and permanent fix, with versions bullets
    9 Preventive actions Owned, dated actions for the process gaps table
    10 Verification How you proved the fix works and how you will know it holds bullets
    11 Appendix Evidence excerpts: stacks, log lines, tool output, references as needed

    2.2 Trigger versus root cause¶

    This is the distinction that separates a senior RCA from a junior one.

    • Trigger: the event or condition that made the latent problem visible at that moment. A traffic spike, a particular input, a config change, a month-end batch, an OS patch.
    • Root cause: the defect or design flaw that made the system fail when triggered. Remove the root cause and the trigger becomes harmless. Remove only the trigger and the system fails again on the next one.
    Wrong (trigger presented as root cause) Right
    "Root cause: unusually high load at month-end." Trigger: month-end load. Root cause: a race in session expiry that needs two concurrent requests on the same session; it only becomes likely at high load.
    "Root cause: a malformed message from the partner bank." Trigger: a 13-character account id. Root cause: the parser writes one byte past a 12-byte buffer and does not validate field lengths.
    "Root cause: the server ran out of memory." That is a symptom. Root cause: a cache with no eviction bound.
    "Root cause: human error during deployment." Root cause: a deployment step that can be done wrong without any check failing. The fix is the check.

    A useful test: "If this condition happens again tomorrow, will the system now survive?" If the answer depends on the condition not happening, you have described the trigger.

    There can be more than one root cause (a defect plus a missing safeguard). And there is often a second level: the technical root cause (the bug) and the process root cause (why review, tests and tools did not catch it). A good RCA names both and fixes both.

    2.3 Evidence¶

    Every claim in the root-cause section is one of:

    • Proven: you have direct evidence - a stack from the dump, a log line, a reproduction that fails before the fix and passes after, a tool report (ASan, TSan, !heap -p -a).
    • Inferred: it follows from proven facts by reasoning you can state.
    • Hypothesis: consistent with the evidence but not proven.

    Label them. "The dump shows" (proven), "therefore" (inferred), "we believe" (hypothesis). An RCA whose root cause is a hypothesis is a preliminary RCA; say so, say what evidence would confirm it, and say what detection you added to get that evidence next time (module 10's ring buffer, asserts, PageHeap on one node). Never present a hypothesis as proven to close an incident faster: if it is wrong, the incident recurs and the next RCA starts with zero credibility.

    Put raw evidence in the appendix (stacks, log excerpts, tool reports, trimmed to the relevant lines) and reference it from the body: "(Appendix A.2)".

    2.4 5-whys¶

    The 5-whys is a way to walk from the symptom to the process gap by asking "why?" repeatedly. Five is a guideline, not a rule; stop when the next answer is outside your control or is a process you can change.

    Rules that make it work:

    • Each answer must be supported by evidence or be clearly a process statement.
    • Follow the most important branch; note other branches as contributing factors.
    • Do not stop at the bug. "Why did the bug exist?" leads to "why did review not catch it?" and "why did tests not catch it?". That is where prevention lives.
    • Never end at a person. "Because the developer made a mistake" is always followed by "why did the system let a mistake reach production?".

    2.5 Tone for a banking client¶

    • Factual, calm, precise. No adjectives that judge ("unfortunately", "silly", "minor glitch"). Numbers with units and timestamps with timezone.
    • Own it. "A defect in our session handling code" - not "an unexpected condition", not "a third-party library" unless you proved it.
    • No blame, no names. Internal names never appear. Teams can appear as roles ("vendor support", "bank operations").
    • No excessive apology, no defensiveness. One sentence acknowledging impact is right; three paragraphs read as evasive.
    • Explain technical terms once, in plain words. The operations head must follow the summary; the architect must find the mechanism precise. Put the plain version in the summary and the precise version in section 4.
    • Commit only to what you control. Dates for your actions; "recommend" for the bank's.
    • Be explicit about data. Banks care first whether any transaction was lost, duplicated or posted wrongly. State it, and state how you verified it (reconciliation query, count).
    • Same terms everywhere. If the summary says "fund transfer service", the timeline does not switch to "FT module" and "xferd".

    2.6 Preventive actions that actually prevent¶

    A preventive action is good when it is specific, owned, dated and verifiable:

    Weak Strong
    Improve code review. Add "every buffer write is bounded by the destination size" to the review checklist for parser code; owner: dev lead; by 2026-11-15; done when the checklist is in the merge-request template.
    Add more testing. Add a libFuzzer target for the ISO 8583 parser running 30 minutes nightly; owner: Shakir; by 2026-11-30; done when the job runs and its first week's results are reviewed.
    Monitor memory. Alert when RSS of the service grows more than 200 MB in 6 hours; owner: support lead; by 2026-10-25.

    Classify actions by what they do: eliminate the bug class (RAII, bounded types), detect it earlier (sanitizers in CI, fuzzing, static analysis), contain it (input validation, flags, restart limits), diagnose faster (logging, dump config). Module 13 is the catalogue.

    2.7 Verification¶

    "We fixed it" needs proof, in two parts:

    • The fix fixes it. A reproduction (test, fuzz input, stress run) that fails on the old build and passes on the new one. Sanitizer runs clean. For races: TSan clean, stress test passes N iterations that previously failed within M.
    • The fix holds in production. A metric or log signal you will watch, for how long, and what value means success: "zero occurrences of event sess.uaf_guard and zero crashes over 30 days including month-end".

    3. Code¶

    There is no program for this module. The "code" is the two complete RCAs below. They are illustrative: the bank, the system, the people, the numbers and the timestamps are invented for teaching, though the bug mechanisms are real and match the programs in code/. Use them as templates. Copy the structure, not the facts.

    3.1 Example RCA 1: heap memory corruption¶

    ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE - fictional bank, system and data, written as a template.

    Root Cause Analysis - INC-2026-0917: Payment Gateway Adapter process terminations

    Field Value
    Incident id INC-2026-0917
    Client Example Bank Ltd. (fictional)
    Service Payment Gateway Adapter (PGA), version 4.12.3
    Environment Production, data centre DC1, nodes PGA-1 and PGA-2 (Linux, RHEL 8)
    Severity Sev-2 (degraded; partial outage with automatic retry)
    Incident window 2026-09-17 11:02 IST to 2026-09-17 14:40 IST (mitigated); permanent fix 2026-09-21
    Document status Final, version 1.1 (preliminary v1.0 issued 2026-09-18)
    Prepared by Vendor product support, core engineering
    Reviewed by Vendor engineering head; bank IT application owner

    1. Executive summary

    On 17 September 2026, between 11:02 and 14:40 IST, the Payment Gateway Adapter process terminated unexpectedly 14 times across two nodes. Each termination interrupted in-flight requests for a few seconds until the process restarted automatically. 312 payment requests failed and were retried successfully by the upstream channel; no transaction was lost, duplicated or posted with a wrong amount (verified by reconciliation, section 2).

    The root cause is a defect in the PGA's message parser: when a beneficiary reference field is longer than 35 characters, the parser writes past the end of a memory buffer, corrupting the memory allocator's internal data. The process then terminates later, when the allocator detects the corruption. The defect has existed since version 4.10.0 (released March 2026); it was triggered on 17 September when a partner bank began sending a longer reference format.

    Service was restored at 14:40 IST by rejecting over-length references at the input boundary. The permanent fix (version 4.12.4) bounds the copy and validates every field length; it was deployed on 21 September. We are adding fuzz testing and memory-error detection to our build pipeline to find this class of defect before release.

    4. Root cause

    4.1 The defect. parse_beneficiary() copies the beneficiary reference into a 36-byte field of the PaymentMessage structure (35 characters plus terminator). The copy loop is bounded by the length of the input field, not the destination:

    // version 4.10.0 - 4.12.3
for (size_t i = 0; i < field.len; ++i)      // field.len comes from the wire
    msg->beneficiary_ref[i] = field.data[i];
msg->beneficiary_ref[field.len] = '\0';

    For a 40-character reference this writes 4 characters and a terminator (5 bytes) past the end of beneficiary_ref. PaymentMessage is allocated on the heap and beneficiary_ref is its last member, so the excess bytes overwrite the allocator's header of the next heap block.

    4.2 Why the process terminated later and in different places. The glibc allocator stores the size of each block in a header just before it. The overwritten header is not checked when it is written; it is checked when that neighbouring block is freed or merged. That happens in whatever code next frees memory near the corrupted block - often in a different thread, milliseconds to seconds later. The allocator then detects an impossible size and stops the process deliberately (malloc(): invalid next size, free(): invalid next size (fast)). This is why the 14 core files show 5 different stacks, none of them in the parser (Appendix A.1). Heap corruption typically presents this way: the crash site shows where the damage was found, not where it was done.

    4.3 Evidence chain.

    1. Proven - all 14 terminations are SIGABRT raised by glibc's heap consistency checks (core files, p $_siginfo, abort messages in the journal). Appendix A.1.
    2. Proven - in all 14 cases, the same thread (matching kernel thread id) processed a request with a beneficiary reference of 36-40 characters between 0.1 and 1.8 seconds before the termination; no termination occurred without such a request. Appendix A.2.
    3. Proven - in 3 analysed cores, the heap block adjacent to a PaymentMessage contains the ASCII tail of a beneficiary reference where the allocator header should be (x/8xg on the block). Appendix A.1.
    4. Proven - the test build with AddressSanitizer reports heap-buffer-overflow ... WRITE of size 1 ... in parse_beneficiary payment_parser.cpp:212 for a 40-character reference, located 0 bytes after the 168-byte PaymentMessage region. Appendix A.3.
    5. Proven - with version 4.12.4, the same test and 10 million fuzzed messages produce no sanitizer reports (section 10).

    5. Trigger

    At 10:30 IST on 17 September a partner bank began sending beneficiary references of up to 40 characters, permitted by its updated message specification. References longer than 35 characters had not appeared in production before that day (checked against 90 days of logs). The trigger is the new input; the root cause is the parser's failure to bound its copy and validate field length.

    6. Contributing factors

    • No input validation at the gateway boundary for field lengths; the parser trusted the wire.
    • The parser change in 4.10.0 was reviewed, but the review checklist had no item for bounded copies, and unit tests used only valid-length references.
    • Memory-error detection (AddressSanitizer) and fuzz testing were not part of the release pipeline.
    • The restart alert threshold (5 per hour) delayed engagement by 72 minutes after the first termination.
    • The partner bank's format change was not communicated to the vendor in advance.

    7. 5-whys

    1. Why did requests fail? The PGA process terminated 14 times.
    2. Why did it terminate? The memory allocator detected corrupted heap metadata and aborted.
    3. Why was the heap metadata corrupted? parse_beneficiary wrote up to 5 bytes past a 36-byte field for references longer than 35 characters.
    4. Why did the parser write past the field? The copy was bounded by the input length, not the destination size, and field lengths were not validated against the specification.
    5. Why did this reach production? Tests covered only valid-length inputs, review had no bounded-copy check, and the pipeline had no fuzzing or AddressSanitizer stage that would have found it in seconds.

    8. Resolution

    • Mitigation (17 Sep, 14:35): input rule at the gateway rejects beneficiary references longer than 35 characters with business error PGA-422, so the partner bank's system receives a clear rejection instead of a dropped connection. Partner bank informed at 14:50.
    • Permanent fix (version 4.12.4, 21 Sep): the parser validates every field against the maximum length in the specification and rejects the message if exceeded; copies use a bounded helper that cannot exceed the destination size; beneficiary_ref changed to a fixed-capacity string type with checked assignment. All 23 other fields of the parser reviewed for the same pattern; 2 further unbounded copies found and fixed (not yet triggered).
    • Extended format: support for 40-character references is scheduled for version 4.13 as a planned change, with the partner bank's specification.

    9. Preventive actions

    # Action Type Owner Due Status
    P1 Add libFuzzer targets for all wire-format parsers; run 30 min per target nightly; crashes block release Detect Vendor core engineering 2026-10-31 In progress
    P2 Add AddressSanitizer + UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer build and full regression run to CI for every merge Detect Vendor build team 2026-10-15 Done
    P3 Add "every copy bounded by destination size; every wire field length validated" to the parser review checklist Eliminate Vendor dev lead 2026-09-30 Done
    P4 Replace raw char[] fields in message structures with a bounded string type across the PGA Eliminate Vendor core engineering 2026-12-15 Planned
    P5 Lower restart alert threshold to 2 per 10 minutes and page on-call Diagnose Bank operations (recommended) 2026-10-05 Done
    P6 Agree notice period for partner message format changes Contain Bank integration team (recommended) 2026-10-31 Open

    10. Verification

    • The reproduction test (40-character reference) fails under AddressSanitizer on 4.12.3 and passes on 4.12.4. Added to the regression suite as test_beneficiary_ref_overlength.
    • 10 million fuzzer-generated messages on 4.12.4 with AddressSanitizer: no reports.
    • Production: zero terminations from 21 Sep to 21 Oct, including month-end. Watched via restart count and the new pga.field_rejected counter (14 over-length references rejected cleanly during the period, all from the same partner, before their 4.13 switch).

    3.2 Example RCA 2: deadlock¶

    ILLUSTRATIVE EXAMPLE - fictional bank, system and data, written as a template.

    Root Cause Analysis - INC-2026-1003: Core Banking Application Server unresponsive

    Field Value
    Incident id INC-2026-1003
    Client Example Bank Ltd. (fictional)
    Service Core Banking Application Server (CBAS), version 7.3.1, RPC-over-TCP
    Environment Production, node CBAS-APP-3 (Windows Server 2022)
    Severity Sev-1 (all branch transactions on one cluster blocked)
    Incident window 2026-10-03 18:47 IST to 2026-10-03 19:21 IST
    Document status Final, version 1.0
    Prepared by Vendor product support, core engineering
    Reviewed by Vendor engineering head; bank IT head of applications

    1. Executive summary

    On 3 October 2026 between 18:47 and 19:21 IST, the core banking application server on node CBAS-APP-3 stopped responding. Branch users connected to that node (61 branches in the western region) could not complete transactions for 34 minutes; other nodes were unaffected. The server was restarted at 19:19 after a memory snapshot was taken, and service resumed at 19:21. No transaction was lost, duplicated or posted wrongly; transactions in progress at 18:47 were rolled back by the database and were re-entered by the branches.

    The root cause is a deadlock: two internal locks in the server could be taken in opposite orders by two different operations - the end-of-day cash position update and the session timeout sweeper. When both ran at the same moment on the same branch session, each waited for the other forever, and all later requests queued behind them. The defect was introduced in version 7.3.0 (August 2026). It was triggered on 3 October by an extended end-of-day window that overlapped the sweeper's schedule. The fix (version 7.3.2) enforces a single lock order; it was deployed on 6 October.

    4. Root cause

    4.1 The defect. The server protects two pieces of shared state with two locks:

    • cash_lock - protects the branch cash position table.
    • session_lock (per branch session) - protects the session object.

    Version 7.3.0 changed the cash position update so that, while holding cash_lock, it also takes the branch's session_lock to stamp the session with the new position. The session sweeper, which already existed, takes session_lock and then, for sessions with open cash drawers, takes cash_lock to release the drawer:

    Cash position update (thread A)        Session sweeper (thread B)
  lock(cash_lock)                        lock(session_lock[branch 0417])
  ...                                    ...
  lock(session_lock[branch 0417])  <-->  lock(cash_lock)
  waits forever for B                    waits forever for A

    If both run for the same branch session at the same moment, each holds the lock the other needs. Neither can proceed and neither ever releases. This is a lock-order inversion deadlock. All further requests that need cash_lock (every cash transaction) or that branch's session queue behind them, and within about a minute every worker thread is blocked.

    4.2 Evidence chain.

    1. Proven - process CPU near 0% with all threads alive, and two dumps 60 s apart show identical stacks for every blocked thread: nothing was progressing (Appendix B.1, B.2). This rules out an overload or slow database.
    2. Proven - !locks shows cash_lock (a critical section) owned by thread 0x1a2c, which is waiting in EnterCriticalSection for session_lock of branch session 0417, owned by thread 0x2f40. Thread 0x2f40 is waiting for cash_lock. That is a cycle (Appendix B.1).
    3. Proven - thread 0x1a2c's stack is in CashPosition::apply; thread 0x2f40's stack is in SessionSweeper::expire. Their call paths take the locks in opposite orders (source review of 7.3.1, cited lines in Appendix B.1).
    4. Proven - 46 other worker threads are waiting on cash_lock (!locks contention list), explaining the total loss of service.
    5. Proven - in test, a stress harness running both operations against one session deadlocks within 3 minutes on 7.3.1; ThreadSanitizer (Linux test build) reports lock-order-inversion (potential deadlock) naming the same two functions, even on runs that did not hang. On 7.3.2: no deadlock in 8 hours and no TSan report (Appendix B.3, section 10).

    5. Trigger

    The sweeper and the cash position update had never run at the same time in production: the update normally finishes by 18:30 and the sweeper's cycles were at 18:32 and 18:47. On 3 October the bank extended the end-of-day window by 30 minutes, so the cash position update ran from 18:30 and overlapped the 18:47 sweep. The overlap made the deadlock possible; it occurred when both reached branch session 0417 at the same moment. The extension is the trigger; the opposite lock order is the root cause. Without a fix, any overlap - for example on a slow month-end - could cause it again.

    6. Contributing factors

    • No documented global lock order existed, so the 7.3.0 change could not be checked against one.
    • Concurrency tests ran the cash update and the sweeper separately, never together.
    • ThreadSanitizer was not part of CI (the Windows production build has no TSan; the Linux test build supports it but was not run under it).
    • The server had no lock-wait watchdog; a hang was detected by queue-depth alert after 5 minutes and diagnosed only by dump.
    • Restart procedure was correct (dumps taken first), which made the 30-minute diagnosis possible.

    7. 5-whys

    1. Why did branches lose service? All worker threads on CBAS-APP-3 were blocked.
    2. Why were they blocked? They waited for cash_lock, held by a thread that was itself blocked.
    3. Why was that thread blocked forever? It waited for a session lock held by the sweeper, which waited for cash_lock: a lock-order cycle.
    4. Why could the locks be taken in opposite orders? The 7.3.0 change added a nested lock without a defined global lock order to check against.
    5. Why was it not caught before release? No documented lock order, no test running both operations concurrently, and no ThreadSanitizer stage, which would have reported the inversion from a single non-hanging run.

    8. Resolution

    • Mitigation (3 Oct): dumps captured, process restarted at 19:19. Until the fix, the sweeper schedule was moved to avoid the end-of-day window (configuration change, 3 Oct 22:00).
    • Permanent fix (version 7.3.2, 6 Oct): the cash position update no longer takes session_lock while holding cash_lock; it records the session stamp after releasing cash_lock. A global lock order (session before cash) is documented and enforced in debug and test builds by a lock- rank checker that fails immediately on an out-of-order acquisition. The sweeper's schedule restored.
    • All 14 other code paths that take two or more locks reviewed against the new order; one more latent inversion found (report generation versus branch close) and fixed in 7.3.2.

    9. Preventive actions

    # Action Type Owner Due Status
    P1 Document the global lock order in the architecture guide; review checklist item "new nested lock respects the order" Eliminate Vendor architect 2026-10-20 Done
    P2 Lock-rank checker enabled in all debug and test builds Detect Vendor core engineering 2026-10-06 Done (in 7.3.2)
    P3 ThreadSanitizer stage in CI on the Linux test build for the concurrency test suite Detect Vendor build team 2026-10-31 In progress

    10. Verification

    • Stress harness: 7.3.1 deadlocks within 3 minutes (5 of 5 runs); 7.3.2 runs 8 hours without deadlock (3 runs).
    • ThreadSanitizer: lock-order-inversion report on 7.3.1; clean on 7.3.2 for the full concurrency suite.
    • Lock-rank checker: deliberately reverting the fix in a test build makes the checker abort at the first out-of-order acquisition with both lock names, on every run.
    • Production: no hang and zero lock-watchdog events from 6 Oct to 5 Nov, including month-end (31 Oct) with the original sweeper schedule.

    4. Under the hood (senior-level)¶

    What a bank does with your RCA. The application owner reads the summary and impact; IT risk checks that the root cause is distinguished from the trigger and that preventive actions are owned and dated; the architecture team reads section 4 for whether the explanation is technically credible; audit later checks that the actions were closed. Regulators in many jurisdictions expect banks to report significant IT incidents and to show root-cause analysis and remediation; your document is the source for that. Write as if each section will be read by a different, sceptical person - because it will.

    Causal models beyond 5-whys. 5-whys is linear; real incidents are usually a combination of conditions (a defect, a missing check, a trigger, slow detection). Two tools seniors use:

    • Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram: branches for code, design, process, testing, environment, people/communication; list causes on each. Good for finding contributing factors.
    • Fault tree / "necessary and sufficient": list the conditions that were all necessary (remove any one and the incident does not happen). Each necessary condition is a place a preventive action can break the chain. In RCA 2: the opposite lock order (defect), the overlap (trigger), the same session at the same moment (timing), no watchdog (slow detection). Breaking the first prevents every future occurrence; breaking the second only this one.

    Why "proven" is a high bar for memory corruption. The crash site is not the bug site (module 03), so the dump alone rarely proves the cause. The chain usually needs: the dump showing what was corrupted (header bytes, freed-pattern), correlation showing which input preceded it, and a reproduction under ASan/PageHeap showing which line wrote it. Two of the three give a strong inference; all three give proof. Say which you have.

    Why "proven" is achievable for deadlocks. A deadlock is a static state; the dump shows every thread, every lock owner and the cycle. Two dumps prove that nothing moves. That is why the deadlock RCA can state the root cause as proven within an hour, while a heap-corruption RCA often needs a reproduction first.

    Preliminary versus final. Issue a preliminary RCA when the contract requires it, even if the root cause is not yet proven: impact, timeline, mitigation, current hypothesis clearly labelled, and what you are doing to confirm it. The final RCA replaces it; keep a version history so the reader can see what changed and why.

    5. In production¶

    • Start the RCA during the incident. The incident log of module 11 (timestamped actions and observations) becomes section 3 almost unchanged. Reconstructing a timeline from memory a week later is unreliable and slow.
    • Have someone who did not work the incident review it. The reviewer checks: can the summary be understood without the rest; is every root-cause claim backed by appendix evidence; is the trigger separated; does each preventive action have owner, date and a "done when".
    • The "could not reproduce" RCA. Sometimes you cannot prove the root cause. The honest version: evidence gathered, most likely mechanism with its supporting evidence, what else was ruled out and how, mitigation in place, diagnostics added so the next occurrence will be conclusive, and the RCA stays open. Banks accept this far more readily than a confident wrong answer that fails a month later.
    • Repeat incidents. Before writing, search previous RCAs for the same stack signature or component. If this is a recurrence, say so in the summary and explain why the previous fix did not hold. Hiding it is found out in the client's quarterly review.
    • Third-party causes. If the bug is in a library, OS or database, prove it (a minimal reproduction, the vendor's bug id). Even then, the RCA usually has a part you own: why your system was exposed, why there was no containment.
    • What you leave out. Internal blame, speculation not labelled as such, customer personal data (mask account numbers in evidence), and full dumps (reference them by id and hash; they stay in the secure store).
    • Shakir's context. For Extrieve-style deployments at HDFC Bank or Kotak Mahindra Bank, the bank usually has its own RCA template and review board. Map this module's sections onto their template; the content requirements are the same. Keep the WinDbg excerpts short (the 6 lines that prove it, not 200 lines of !analyze -v).

    6. Try it¶

    1. Easy - trigger or root cause. For each, label trigger, root cause or symptom, and rewrite the wrong ones: "server ran out of file descriptors"; "a new partner sent an empty amount field"; "connections were not closed on the error path"; "Windows patch Tuesday"; "an unordered_map was modified by two threads without a lock". Done when each has a label and a one-line justification using the "will it survive tomorrow" test.
    2. Easy - critique a summary. Rewrite this summary for a bank: "Due to an unexpected issue the xferd module crashed several times. The developer has fixed it. Root cause was high load. We apologise for any inconvenience caused and regret the same." Done when the rewrite states what failed, impact with numbers, the root cause, trigger, fix and data integrity in under 8 sentences.
    3. Medium - RCA from the course's deadlock. Run m10_deadlock, take two gcores (module 11 evidence script), and write sections 4 (root cause with evidence chain), 5, 7 and 10 of an RCA for it, with your real LWPs and stacks in an appendix. Done when every claim in section 4 is labelled proven, inferred or hypothesis and points to an appendix item.
    4. Medium - RCA from the heap overflow. Using m04_heap_overflow (no ASan core plus an ASan reproduction), write a complete RCA following RCA 1 as a template, inventing a plausible trigger. Done when a reviewer can find the root cause, the trigger and three preventive actions that are specific, owned, dated and verifiable.
    5. Hard - the unprovable one. Write a preliminary RCA for m10_data_race as if you had only one core showing a crash in _Rb_tree_insert_and_rebalance and no reproduction yet. Done when the document makes no unproven claim as fact, lists what was ruled out, and names the diagnostic you added to make the next occurrence conclusive.
    6. Hard - your own incident. Take a real incident from your work (anonymise the bank, people and numbers) and rewrite its RCA in this structure. Done when you can present it in 5 minutes in an interview, including the trigger-versus-root-cause distinction and one preventive action that would have stopped it.

    7. Interview questions¶

    Q: What is the difference between the trigger and the root cause? A: The trigger is the event that exposed the problem at that time - a traffic spike, an unusual input, a schedule overlap. The root cause is the defect that made the system fail under that event; removing it makes the trigger harmless. Test: if the trigger recurs tomorrow, will the system survive? If not, you have only described the trigger.

    Q: Walk me through the structure of an RCA you would send a bank. A: Header, executive summary in plain words, impact with numbers and data-integrity statement, timestamped timeline with sources, root cause with a labelled evidence chain, trigger, contributing factors, 5-whys down to the process gap, resolution (mitigation and permanent fix), owned and dated preventive actions, verification of the fix and of its holding in production, and an appendix of evidence excerpts.

    Q: How do you write an RCA when you could not prove the root cause? A: Issue it as preliminary: state the evidence, the most likely mechanism labelled as a hypothesis, what was ruled out and how, the mitigation in place, and the diagnostics added so the next occurrence is conclusive. Keep it open with a review date. A confident wrong root cause costs far more credibility when the incident recurs.

    Q: What makes a preventive action good? A: It is specific, owned, dated and verifiable, and it addresses the process gap, not only the bug: eliminating the bug class, detecting it earlier, containing it or diagnosing it faster. "Add a libFuzzer target for the parser to nightly CI by 30 Nov, owner X, done when it runs" is an action; "improve testing" is not.

    Q: Why does a heap-corruption RCA usually need a reproduction while a deadlock RCA does not? A: The heap corruption is detected later, in other code, so the dump shows where the damage was found, not who did it; you need correlation plus an ASan or PageHeap reproduction to prove the writing line. A deadlock is a frozen state: the dump shows every waiter, every owner and the cycle, and two dumps prove nothing is moving.

    Q: What tone do you use with a banking client? A: Factual, calm and owning: precise numbers and timestamps with timezone, "a defect in our code", no internal names or blame, one sentence of acknowledgement rather than repeated apology, plain words in the summary and precision in the technical section, and an explicit statement about whether any transaction was lost, duplicated or mis-posted and how that was verified.

    Q: How far should the 5-whys go? A: Past the bug, to the process that let it ship: why review, tests and tooling did not catch it. Stop when the next answer is outside your control or is a process you can change. Never end at a person; "a developer made a mistake" is always followed by "why did the system allow it to reach production?".

    8. Recap¶

    • An RCA: header, summary, impact, timeline, root cause with evidence, trigger, contributing factors, 5-whys, resolution, preventive actions, verification, appendix.
    • The trigger is why it happened that day; the root cause is why the system could fail at all. Test: will it survive the trigger tomorrow?
    • Label every claim as proven, inferred or hypothesis; evidence goes in the appendix.
    • Technical root cause plus process root cause: the bug and why it shipped.
    • Banking tone: factual, owning, blameless, numbers and timezones, explicit data-integrity statement, plain summary.
    • Preventive actions are specific, owned, dated, verifiable; classify as eliminate, detect, contain, diagnose.
    • Verification has two parts: the fix fixes it (fails before, passes after) and the fix holds (a metric over a period including high-risk windows).
    • Heap corruption RCAs need correlation plus reproduction; deadlock RCAs are proven by two dumps.
    • Preliminary RCAs are honest about uncertainty and add diagnostics; they stay open.
