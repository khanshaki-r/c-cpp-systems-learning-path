    Step 09. Metrics, logging, STATS, TTL, and KEYS revisited¶

    Time: ~7 hours Week: 5 (Nov 03 – Nov 09) Read first: 04_Concurrency: 07_atomics_and_the_memory_model.md (memory_order_relaxed and when it is enough), 10_cache_lines_false_sharing_performance.md, 04_shared_mutex_and_readers_writers.md (writer starvation, long readers). 02_Modern_CPP: 13_cpp17_features_tour.md (std::optional, structured bindings), 03_raii_the_core_idea.md (RAII for things that are not memory: a counter guard). 07_Databases_Internals: 13_caching_and_redis.md (TTL, lazy and active expiry, why KEYS is banned in production Redis). 05_Memory_Debugging_RCA: 08_valgrind_and_sanitizers.md (UBSan). 06_Linux_Toolchain_DevOps: 07_debugging_and_profiling_tools_on_linux.md.

    1. Goal¶

    Make the server observable and finish the protocol.

    • Metrics: atomic counters for connections, commands, bytes, and a latency histogram of command execution time.
    • STATS returns them; the acceptor logs one throughput line every 10 seconds (--stats-interval).
    • EXPIRE and TTL: per-key time-to-live with lazy expiry on read plus a once-a-second purge.
    • connections_active that is correct on every exit path, via a small RAII guard.
    • Revisit the two pieces that already exist: the logger (step 05) and KEYS prefix (steps 04 and 05), this time with their production costs.
    • The -ERR not implemented yet branch disappears. The protocol in 00_spec.md is now fully implemented.

    In plain words: a server you cannot see into is a server you cannot run. At 3 AM the first three questions are "is it up, how busy is it, how slow is it", and STATS plus one log line every ten seconds answer all three without a debugger. TTL is the feature that turns a map into a cache: "keep this for 60 seconds, then forget it".

    2. Design¶

    2.1 Metrics: relaxed atomics, no locks¶

    worker threads (N)                      acceptor thread              any client
  ++commands_total  (relaxed)             every 10 s: read           STATS: render()
  ++bytes_in / bytes_out                  commands_total, diff,        reads every counter
  histogram.record(latency)               log ops/s and p99            (relaxed loads)
  connections_active +1 / -1 (guard)
    • Every counter is independent. Nobody needs commands_total ordered relative to bytes_out, and no other data is published through a counter. So memory_order_relaxed is correct: each fetch_add is still atomic (no lost increments), it just does not act as a fence. On x86-64, a relaxed fetch_add and a seq_cst one compile to the same lock xadd; the difference is what the compiler may reorder around it, and on ARM it is a real difference in barriers.
    • No mutex anywhere in Metrics. That rules out a whole bug class: no lock-order inversion with the store lock, no contention between STATS and workers.
    • A STATS snapshot is not atomic as a whole. Counters may move between two loads, so connections_active and commands_total in one reply are not from one instant. For monitoring that is fine; say so rather than pretending otherwise.
    • False sharing, honestly. All counters sit next to each other in one struct, so every worker's fetch_add pulls the same 64-byte cache line into its core in exclusive state. With 4 to 16 workers doing one network round trip per command, the syscalls dominate and this does not show up in a profile. If it ever did, the fixes are alignas(64) per hot counter, or per-thread counters summed on read (what the Linux kernel does with per-CPU counters). Measure first (see 04_Concurrency/10_cache_lines_false_sharing_performance.md).

    2.2 The latency histogram¶

    Sixteen buckets on a roughly logarithmic scale, upper bounds in microseconds:

    <=50 <=100 <=200 <=500 <=1ms <=2ms <=5ms <=10ms <=20ms <=50ms <=100ms <=200ms <=500ms <=1s <=5s  >5s
    • record() finds the bucket with a linear scan of 15 constants (cheaper than it sounds: the array is 120 bytes, one or two cache lines) and does three relaxed fetch_adds.
    • percentile_us(p) walks the cumulative counts to the bucket holding rank ceil(p * count) and returns its upper bound. So "p99 = 100 us" means "99% of commands took at most 100 us". You cannot report finer than the bucket, and you should not pretend to.
    • Why not store every sample and sort? Memory grows without bound, and a sort under load is not free. HdrHistogram (used by many load tools) is the production-grade version of this idea with configurable precision; ours is the 60-line version.
    • What is measured: server-side execution of the store operation only, from just before execute_command to just after. It excludes parsing, the socket write and network time. Small numbers here and large numbers in load_gen mean the time is in the network, the kernel or the queue for a worker, not in the store. That split is the point of measuring both.

    2.3 TTL: lazy expiry plus a periodic purge¶

    struct Entry {
    std::string value;
    std::optional<Clock::time_point> expires_at;   // nullopt = lives forever
};
    • steady_clock, not system_clock. A TTL is an interval. If NTP moves the wall clock back an hour, system_clock would make every key live an hour longer.
    • Lazy expiry: every reader checks is_expired(entry, now) and reports "not found". Readers hold only a shared lock, so they cannot erase; they just do not see the entry.
    • Active expiry: the acceptor calls store.purge_expired() once a second. It takes the exclusive lock and erases every expired entry. Without it, keys that are never read again stay in memory forever.
    • SET clears the TTL (like Redis). EXPIRE k 0 or negative deletes at once. DEL on an expired-but-unpurged key erases it and returns 0 (it was not live).
    • TTL rounds up (std::chrono::ceil), so a key with 0.3 s left reports 1, not 0. 0 would read as "expires now" and is easy to confuse with "no TTL".
    • Clock read outside the lock. Clock::now() is a vDSO call, cheap but not free; doing it before taking the lock keeps the critical section to the hash lookup.

    The trade-off to be able to state: purge_expired() is O(total keys) under the exclusive lock, once a second. With a million keys that is a pause of the order of milliseconds during which no writer or reader runs (measure it on your machine before quoting a number). Two better designs:

    1. Sampling (what Redis does): keep a separate set of keys that have a TTL; each cycle, test a small random sample and erase the expired ones; repeat if a large fraction was expired. Bounded work per cycle, probabilistic memory bound.
    2. An expiry index: a min-heap or std::multimap<time_point, key>; purge pops from the front while front <= now. O(expired * log n) per cycle, at the cost of extra memory and of keeping the index in sync on SET/DEL/EXPIRE.

    v1.0.0 uses the simple full scan because it is obviously correct and the key counts in the benchmarks are small. Say exactly that.

    2.4 KEYS prefix, revisited¶

    KEYS has been there since step 04 (store) and step 05 (command): collect matching keys under a shared lock, release, sort. Two production problems:

    • It is O(n) in the total number of keys, not in the number of matches. An unordered_map has no order, so a prefix query must look at every key.
    • It holds the shared lock for the whole scan. Other readers are unaffected, but every writer waits for the full scan. And libstdc++'s std::shared_mutex on Linux wraps pthread_rwlock_t with glibc's default reader-preferring policy (04_Concurrency/04_shared_mutex_and_readers_writers.md): a steady stream of GETs and KEYS overlapping each other can keep the reader count above zero indefinitely, and a SET waits until there is a gap. Long readers plus reader preference is how writer starvation happens.

    This is why Redis documentation warns against KEYS in production and provides SCAN. If asked how you would fix it, in order of effort:

    1. Cap the reply (KEYS prefix LIMIT n) and document it. Cheap, honest.
    2. SCAN cursor [MATCH prefix] [COUNT n]: return up to n keys and a cursor; the client loops. With an unordered_map the cursor can be a bucket index (iterate bucket(i)), which is stable across calls unless a rehash happens; Redis solves that with reverse-binary cursor iteration. Each call holds the lock for a bounded time.
    3. A second, ordered index (std::map<std::string, ...> or a trie) so a prefix query is lower_bound(prefix) then walk while the prefix matches: O(log n + matches). Costs memory and a second structure to update on every write.

    2.5 Logging, revisited¶

    The logger arrived in step 05. What matters now that the server is concurrent and long-lived:

    • One line, one fwrite, under one mutex. write() formats the whole line first, then locks only for the fwrite + fflush. Lines from different threads never interleave, and the critical section does no formatting.
    • The level check happens before formatting. log::debug(...) with the level at info costs one relaxed atomic load and a compare; the arguments are never streamed. That is why per-connection events are at debug: a busy server would otherwise spend measurable time formatting and writing thousands of lines a second.
    • localtime_r, not localtime. localtime returns a pointer to a static buffer shared by all threads: a data race TSan would report.
    • gettid() in the line: the kernel thread id, the same number top -H, ps -L and gdb's info threads show. When a worker is stuck, the log tells you which LWP to attach to.
    • stderr, not a file. The process does not manage log files, rotation or disk-full. Under systemd, stderr goes to the journal; in a container, to the container runtime's log driver. This is the twelve-factor convention, and it is what you want in production.
    • Not async-signal-safe. It allocates and locks. Never call it from a signal handler (step 08, item 6).

    3. Files to create or change¶

    include/tcpkv/metrics.hpp (final version)¶

    Step 08 created the file with LatencyHistogram. Add #include <string> and the Metrics struct below the histogram:

    // All server counters live here. Plain atomics with relaxed ordering: each counter is
// independent and we never need one to be ordered relative to another.
struct Metrics {
    std::atomic<std::uint64_t> connections_accepted{0};
    std::atomic<std::uint64_t> connections_active{0};
    std::atomic<std::uint64_t> connections_rejected{0};  // "server busy": queue was full
    std::atomic<std::uint64_t> commands_total{0};
    std::atomic<std::uint64_t> commands_failed{0};  // parse errors
    std::atomic<std::uint64_t> bytes_in{0};
    std::atomic<std::uint64_t> bytes_out{0};
    LatencyHistogram command_latency;  // execution time of the store operation, server side
    std::chrono::steady_clock::time_point started_at = std::chrono::steady_clock::now();

    // "name value\n" per line. This is what the STATS command returns.
    std::string render() const;
};

    Metrics is not copyable (atomics and the histogram are not), which is right: there is one set of counters per server, passed by reference.

    src/metrics.cpp (final version)¶

    #include "tcpkv/metrics.hpp"

#include <algorithm>
#include <cmath>
#include <sstream>

namespace tcpkv {

double LatencyHistogram::Snapshot::mean_us() const noexcept {
    return count == 0 ? 0.0 : static_cast<double>(total_us) / static_cast<double>(count);
}

std::uint64_t LatencyHistogram::Snapshot::percentile_us(double p) const noexcept {
    if (count == 0) {
        return 0;
    }
    const double clamped = std::min(std::max(p, 0.0), 1.0);
    const auto target = std::max<std::uint64_t>(
        1, static_cast<std::uint64_t>(std::ceil(clamped * static_cast<double>(count))));
    std::uint64_t cumulative = 0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < kBucketCount; ++i) {
        cumulative += buckets[i];
        if (cumulative >= target) {
            return i < kBoundsUs.size() ? kBoundsUs[i] : kBoundsUs.back();
        }
    }
    return kBoundsUs.back();
}

void LatencyHistogram::Snapshot::merge(const Snapshot& other) noexcept {
    count += other.count;
    total_us += other.total_us;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < kBucketCount; ++i) {
        buckets[i] += other.buckets[i];
    }
}

void LatencyHistogram::record(std::chrono::nanoseconds latency) noexcept {
    const auto signed_us = std::chrono::duration_cast<std::chrono::microseconds>(latency).count();
    const std::uint64_t us = signed_us < 0 ? 0 : static_cast<std::uint64_t>(signed_us);
    std::size_t index = 0;
    while (index < kBoundsUs.size() && us > kBoundsUs[index]) {
        ++index;
    }
    buckets_[index].fetch_add(1, std::memory_order_relaxed);
    count_.fetch_add(1, std::memory_order_relaxed);
    total_us_.fetch_add(us, std::memory_order_relaxed);
}

LatencyHistogram::Snapshot LatencyHistogram::snapshot() const noexcept {
    // Not atomic as a whole: counters may move between loads. For monitoring that is fine.
    Snapshot s;
    s.count = count_.load(std::memory_order_relaxed);
    s.total_us = total_us_.load(std::memory_order_relaxed);
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < kBucketCount; ++i) {
        s.buckets[i] = buckets_[i].load(std::memory_order_relaxed);
    }
    return s;
}

std::string Metrics::render() const {
    const auto uptime = std::chrono::steady_clock::now() - started_at;
    const auto latency = command_latency.snapshot();
    std::ostringstream os;
    os << "uptime_seconds " << std::chrono::duration_cast<std::chrono::seconds>(uptime).count()
       << '\n';
    os << "connections_accepted " << connections_accepted.load(std::memory_order_relaxed) << '\n';
    os << "connections_active " << connections_active.load(std::memory_order_relaxed) << '\n';
    os << "connections_rejected " << connections_rejected.load(std::memory_order_relaxed) << '\n';
    os << "commands_total " << commands_total.load(std::memory_order_relaxed) << '\n';
    os << "commands_failed " << commands_failed.load(std::memory_order_relaxed) << '\n';
    os << "bytes_in " << bytes_in.load(std::memory_order_relaxed) << '\n';
    os << "bytes_out " << bytes_out.load(std::memory_order_relaxed) << '\n';
    os << "latency_count " << latency.count << '\n';
    os << "latency_mean_us " << static_cast<std::uint64_t>(latency.mean_us()) << '\n';
    os << "latency_p50_us " << latency.percentile_us(0.50) << '\n';
    os << "latency_p99_us " << latency.percentile_us(0.99) << '\n';
    os << "latency_p999_us " << latency.percentile_us(0.999) << '\n';
    return os.str();
}

}  // namespace tcpkv

    Details worth a sentence each:

    • std::max<std::uint64_t>(1, ...) makes percentile_us(0.0) mean "the smallest sample" rather than rank 0, which does not exist.
    • duration_cast<microseconds> truncates: a 49.9 us command lands in the <=50 bucket. Negative durations (impossible with steady_clock, but cheap to guard) clamp to 0.
    • total_us can in theory overflow uint64_t (max about 1.8 x 10^19). At one million commands per second averaging 100 us, it grows by 10^8 per second and wraps after about 5,800 years. Unsigned overflow is defined (it wraps), so even then the mean is wrong, not undefined. Do this arithmetic out loud if asked; it is the kind of check seniors make.
    • render() uses ostringstream because STATS is rare and readability wins. Format is Prometheus-like (name value) so a scraper could be written in ten lines.

    include/tcpkv/store.hpp and src/store.cpp (final versions)¶

    Identical to code/include/tcpkv/store.hpp and code/src/store.cpp. Compared with step 04: the value type becomes Entry, three methods are added, and every reader checks expiry.

        // ttl <= 0 deletes the key. Returns false if the key does not exist.
    // Takes Clock::duration (not seconds) so tests can use short TTLs; std::chrono::seconds
    // converts implicitly.
    bool expire(const std::string& key, Clock::duration ttl);

    // -2: key does not exist, -1: exists without expiry, otherwise seconds left (rounded up).
    long long ttl(const std::string& key) const;

    std::size_t size() const;  // includes expired-but-not-yet-purged entries

    // Erases expired entries; returns how many were removed.
    std::size_t purge_expired();

private:
    struct Entry {
        std::string value;
        std::optional<Clock::time_point> expires_at;
    };

    static bool is_expired(const Entry& entry, Clock::time_point now) noexcept {
        return entry.expires_at.has_value() && *entry.expires_at <= now;
    }

    mutable std::shared_mutex mutex_;
    std::unordered_map<std::string, Entry> map_;

    The new and changed methods:

    std::optional<std::string> Store::get(const std::string& key) const {
    const auto now = Clock::now();  // read the clock outside the lock: it is not free
    std::shared_lock<std::shared_mutex> lock(mutex_);
    const auto it = map_.find(key);
    if (it == map_.end() || is_expired(it->second, now)) {
        return std::nullopt;
    }
    return it->second.value;  // copy made under the lock; the caller owns it afterwards
}

void Store::set(std::string key, std::string value) {
    std::unique_lock<std::shared_mutex> lock(mutex_);
    Entry& entry = map_[std::move(key)];
    entry.value = std::move(value);
    entry.expires_at.reset();  // like Redis: SET makes the key persistent again
}

bool Store::del(const std::string& key) {
    const auto now = Clock::now();
    std::unique_lock<std::shared_mutex> lock(mutex_);
    const auto it = map_.find(key);
    if (it == map_.end()) {
        return false;
    }
    const bool was_live = !is_expired(it->second, now);
    map_.erase(it);
    return was_live;
}

bool Store::expire(const std::string& key, Clock::duration ttl) {
    const auto now = Clock::now();
    std::unique_lock<std::shared_mutex> lock(mutex_);
    const auto it = map_.find(key);
    if (it == map_.end() || is_expired(it->second, now)) {
        return false;
    }
    if (ttl.count() <= 0) {
        map_.erase(it);
        return true;
    }
    it->second.expires_at = now + ttl;
    return true;
}

long long Store::ttl(const std::string& key) const {
    const auto now = Clock::now();
    std::shared_lock<std::shared_mutex> lock(mutex_);
    const auto it = map_.find(key);
    if (it == map_.end() || is_expired(it->second, now)) {
        return -2;
    }
    if (!it->second.expires_at) {
        return -1;
    }
    const auto remaining = *it->second.expires_at - now;
    return std::chrono::ceil<std::chrono::seconds>(remaining).count();
}

std::size_t Store::purge_expired() {
    const auto now = Clock::now();
    std::unique_lock<std::shared_mutex> lock(mutex_);
    std::size_t removed = 0;
    for (auto it = map_.begin(); it != map_.end();) {
        if (is_expired(it->second, now)) {
            it = map_.erase(it);  // erase() returns the next valid iterator
            ++removed;
        } else {
            ++it;
        }
    }
    return removed;
}

    exists and keys gain the same is_expired check; in keys the structured binding becomes [key, entry] and the step-04 (void)value; goes away. size() deliberately counts expired-but-unpurged entries: it reports memory, not logical contents, and the tests rely on that to observe the purge.

class ActiveConnectionGuard {
public:
    explicit ActiveConnectionGuard(Metrics& metrics) noexcept : metrics_(metrics) {
        metrics_.connections_active.fetch_add(1, std::memory_order_relaxed);
    }
    ~ActiveConnectionGuard() { metrics_.connections_active.fetch_sub(1, std::memory_order_relaxed); }
    ActiveConnectionGuard(const ActiveConnectionGuard&) = delete;
    ActiveConnectionGuard& operator=(const ActiveConnectionGuard&) = delete;

private:
    Metrics& metrics_;
};

    serve() starts with const ActiveConnectionGuard active_guard(*metrics_);. serve() has five ways out (EOF, QUIT, fatal parse error, idle timeout, stop flag) plus any exception. A manual --active at the end would be wrong on at least one of them. This is RAII for a counter instead of memory, and the ActiveConnectionsDropToZeroAfterClientsLeave test proves it. In C you would write goto out; and hope every path reaches it.

    void Server::log_throughput(std::chrono::steady_clock::time_point now,
                            std::chrono::steady_clock::time_point& last_time,
                            std::uint64_t& last_commands) const {
    const std::uint64_t commands = metrics_.commands_total.load(std::memory_order_relaxed);
    const double seconds = std::chrono::duration<double>(now - last_time).count();
    const std::uint64_t delta = commands - last_commands;
    const auto rate = seconds > 0 ? static_cast<std::uint64_t>(static_cast<double>(delta) / seconds) : 0;
    log::info("stats: ", delta, " commands in last ", static_cast<std::uint64_t>(seconds), " s (",
              rate, " ops/s), active connections ",
              metrics_.connections_active.load(std::memory_order_relaxed), ", p99 ",
              metrics_.command_latency.snapshot().percentile_us(0.99), " us");
    last_time = now;
    last_commands = commands;
}

    The rate is computed from deltas, so it is "ops/s over the last interval", not since start. The p99 is since start (the histogram is cumulative). Know that difference if asked; a windowed p99 would need a histogram per interval, swapped and reset by the acceptor.

    The shutdown log line now reports active connections as well as queued ones.

    A real UBSan finding: EXPIRE with a huge number of seconds¶

    The parser accepts any long long for seconds. execute_command builds std::chrono::seconds(n), and Store::expire takes Clock::duration, which on Linux is nanoseconds in a signed 64-bit integer. The implicit conversion multiplies by 10^9. Any n above about 9.2 x 10^9 (roughly 292 years) overflows: signed overflow, undefined behaviour, triggered by one line from any client. A minimal reproduction of the same conversion, built with -fsanitize=undefined on GCC 13 in WSL, reports:

    /usr/include/c++/13/bits/chrono.h:225:38: runtime error: signed integer overflow:
10000000000 * 1000000000 cannot be represented in type 'long long int'
    #0 ... std::chrono::__duration_cast_impl<...>::__cast<long, std::ratio<1l, 1l> >(...)
    #1 ... std::chrono::duration_cast<std::chrono::duration<long, std::ratio<1l, 1000000000l> >, ...>
    #2 ... std::chrono::duration<long, std::ratio<1l, 1000000000l> >::duration<long, std::ratio<1l, 1l>, void>(...)

    Reproduce it against the server: run ./build/ubsan/tcpkv_server --port 7400, then ./build/ubsan/tcpkv_cli -p 7400 set k v and ./build/ubsan/tcpkv_cli -p 7400 expire k 10000000000. Negative values overflow the same way (expire k -10000000000), before the ttl <= 0 check can run, because the conversion happens at the call.

    None of the tests send such a value, so every CI job is green with this bug present. That is the lesson: sanitizers only judge the inputs you give them. If the parser in your copy of code/ does not already reject these values, fix it in your repo:

    // include/tcpkv/protocol.hpp, in namespace limits
// 100 years. Converted to steady_clock's nanoseconds this is ~3.2e18, below INT64_MAX (~9.2e18),
// and adding it to steady_clock::now() (time since boot) cannot overflow either.
inline constexpr long long kMaxExpireSeconds = 100LL * 365 * 24 * 3600;

    // src/protocol.cpp, in the CommandType::Expire validation
            long long seconds = 0;
            if (!parse_signed(cmd.args[1], seconds) || seconds > limits::kMaxExpireSeconds ||
                seconds < -limits::kMaxExpireSeconds) {
                return fail("invalid expire seconds", false);
            }

    // tests/protocol_test.cpp

TEST(Parser, ExpireSecondsOutOfRangeIsNonFatal) {
    Parser parser;
    parser.feed("EXPIRE k 10000000000\nEXPIRE k -10000000000\nEXPIRE k 3153600000\nPING\n");

    ASSERT_TRUE(ping.has_value());

}

    7. Optional extensions (design notes only; not in v1.0.0)¶

    Do none of these before v1.0.0 is tagged. If you do one after, it is a v1.1.0 with its own tests and README section. Each is a good "what would you do next" answer even if never built.

    • SCAN cursor [MATCH prefix] [COUNT n] (section 2.4). Cursor = bucket index; hold the shared lock per call, not per scan; document that keys inserted during a scan may or may not be returned and that a rehash can cause duplicates (Redis's reverse-binary cursor avoids misses across a rehash; read about it before claiming you would implement it).
    • maxmemory with LRU eviction. Account key.size() + value.size() + overhead per entry (overhead: node, hash, std::string headers; estimate it as a constant and say so). Exact LRU needs an intrusive list updated on every GET, which turns reads into writes and kills the shared lock's benefit. Redis uses approximated LRU by sampling; that is the version compatible with a reader lock.
    • Sharded store. N Store shards, shard = hash(key) % N, each with its own shared_mutex. Writers on different shards stop contending. Costs: KEYS and purge must visit every shard (and cannot give a consistent cross-shard snapshot); multi-key DEL takes several locks (lock in shard order to avoid deadlock). Do it only with a benchmark showing writer contention first (perf, off-cpu time in futex).
    • Prometheus endpoint. A second listener that answers GET /metrics with the render() text plus # TYPE lines. Ten lines of HTTP, and the server plugs into every monitoring stack.
    • Windowed percentiles. Two histograms; the acceptor swaps them every interval and logs the old one's p99, so the log line describes the last 10 seconds, not all time.

    8. What you learned, and what to say in an interview¶

    • Relaxed atomics are correct for independent counters, and you can say why (no data is published through them) and what they cost (a lock-prefixed instruction on x86, plus cache-line traffic if they share a line).
    • Histograms, not averages. Means hide the tail; p99 is what users feel. Bucketed percentiles are upper bounds; say so.
    • Lazy plus active expiry is the standard TTL design; a full-scan purge is the simple version; sampling or an expiry index is the scalable one.
    • KEYS is O(n) under a lock and stalls writers; SCAN or an ordered index is the fix.
    • RAII for counters: the active-connection guard is correct on every exit path by construction.
    • Green sanitizers prove nothing about inputs you never sent. The EXPIRE overflow passed every CI job until one test sent a big number.

    Interview sentence: "Observability is a Metrics struct of relaxed atomics, no locks, and a 16-bucket log-scale latency histogram of command execution time; STATS renders it and the acceptor logs ops/s and p99 every ten seconds. TTL is lazy expiry on read under the shared lock plus a once-a-second purge under the exclusive lock; I know the purge is O(n) and would move to Redis-style sampling or an expiry heap at scale. And one honest story: UBSan in CI was green, but EXPIRE with ten billion seconds overflowed the chrono conversion to nanoseconds; I found it by feeding the UBSan build hostile input by hand, bounded it in the parser, and pinned it with a test."
